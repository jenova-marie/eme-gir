# How to pronounce Sumerian — a Jagersma-based guide

A practical pronunciation reference distilled from **Bram Jagersma,
*A Descriptive Grammar of Sumerian*** (PhD dissertation, Universiteit
Leiden, 2010), Chapter 3 "Phonology" (pp. 31–68). Every claim carries
a `(Jagersma §N.M)` citation so you can verify against the source.

**Scope.** This document tells you how to read a Sumerian word
**out loud**, based on what Jagersma reconstructs for Sumerian as a
living language in the third millennium BCE (~2500–2000 BCE). The
focus is on **Old Sumerian** (= Early Dynastic IIIa–IIIb, 24–25th c.
BCE) pronunciation — the period on which the cheat sheet and Jagersma
himself center their description. Later-period pronunciation drifts
in some specific ways noted in §11 below.

**Companion document.** Use this alongside the cheat sheet
`prompt/SUMERIAN_GRAMMAR.md` (which covers morphology and syntax) and
the convention map `prompt/JAGERSMA_CONVENTIONS.md` (which translates
Jagersma's linguistic notation to Oracc/Eme-gir spelling).

---

## 1. The caveat up front

Sumerian is a dead language with no native speakers; nobody has heard
it spoken in 4,000 years. The pronunciation in this document is
**reconstructed** from indirect evidence:

- Loanwords from Sumerian into Akkadian (and vice versa) (Jagersma
  passim ch. 3)
- The distribution of cuneiform sound signs (which sequences scribes
  bothered to distinguish)
- Greek transcriptions of Sumerian and Akkadian from the Hellenistic
  period (when both languages were still being read aloud in cultic
  contexts) (Jagersma §3.2.1)
- Ebla and Old Akkadian syllabary clues for early phonemes
- Sound-change patterns reconstructed from period-to-period spelling
  differences

Jagersma is conservative about how confident we can be. He warns
(§3.2.1) that "the evidence … is largely complex" and that for many
consonants we have "approximate" descriptions only. He also flags
explicitly in §1.3.2 footnote 3 that **the standard transliteration
is NOT a phonological representation** — it's based on Akkadian
lexical lists from many centuries later, and "the pronunciation of
the word signs is only partly known."

In short: read aloud at your own risk, and consider this document a
best-effort reconstruction rather than a transcript.

## 2. Quick start: a one-screen pronunciation cheat

If you only read one paragraph: **pronounce each letter roughly as in
the IPA / "Italian style," with the following adjustments**:

| Spelling | Sound (~IPA) | Like |
|---|---|---|
| `a` | [a] | Spanish *padre*, Italian *casa* |
| `e` | [e] | Spanish *mesa*, Italian *bene* |
| `i` | [i] | Spanish *si*, Italian *libri* |
| `u` | [u] | Spanish *luna*, Italian *uno* |
| `b d g` | [b d g] | (~3rd mil. these were actually plain voiceless — see §4) |
| `p t k` | [pʰ tʰ kʰ] | English aspirated — *pin, tin, kin* (Jagersma §3.2.2) |
| `m n` | [m n] | English |
| `ŋ` | [ŋ] | English *sing*, but at word beginnings too (Jagersma §3.5.4) |
| `l r` | [l ɾ] | Spanish *l*, single-tap *r* as in *pero* (Jagersma §3.6, §3.7) |
| `s` | [s] | English |
| `š` | [ʃ] | English *sh* |
| `z` | [ts] | Italian *pizza* (Jagersma §3.3.1) |
| `ḫ` | [x] | German *Bach*, Scottish *loch* |
| `j` | [j] | English *y* in *yes* — historical only, mostly lost by Ur III |
| `ʾ` / `÷` | [ʔ] | English *uh-oh*'s middle catch — silent in Oracc spellings |

**Stress** is on the **last syllable** of each phonological word
(Jagersma §3.11). Vowel length is phonemic but not marked in Oracc
spelling — when in doubt, treat written vowels as short.

Worked example: `lugal` → [luˈgal] roughly "loo-GAL" (with stress on
the final syllable, and the `g` actually voiceless in the third
millennium — closer to [kal] than English *gal*; see §4).

## 3. The consonant inventory (Jagersma §3.1)

Sumerian had **sixteen phonemic consonants**, grouped into six
series. Five of them (the four aspirated stops + the affricate `ř`,
plus the glottal stop and `j` and `h`) eroded out of the system
during the third millennium — they're listed because they appear in
Old Sumerian and earlier texts and explain otherwise-puzzling spellings.

| Series | Bilabial | Coronal | Velar | Glottal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voiceless aspirated stops | /p/ | /t/ | /k/ | — |
| Plain voiceless stops (3rd-mil.) → voiced (later) | /b/ | /d/ | /g/ | /ʾ/ (glottal stop) |
| Affricates | — | /z/, /ř/ | — | — |
| Fricatives | — | /s/, /š/ | /ḫ/ | /h/ |
| Nasals | /m/ | /n/ | /ŋ/ | — |
| Liquid / tap / glide | — | /l/, /r/ | /j/ | — |

(Jagersma's full phonological table is reconstructed from §3.2–§3.8.)

## 4. The stop series — the big surprise (Jagersma §3.2)

This is where Sumerian pronunciation differs most sharply from what
the romanization suggests.

### 4.1 What the transliteration says

The signs `b d g` look voiced; `p t k` look voiceless.

### 4.2 What Jagersma reconstructs

In the **third millennium BCE** (i.e. throughout the period this
document covers):

- **`p t k`** were **voiceless ASPIRATED** stops — [pʰ tʰ kʰ] — like
  English *pin, tin, kin* with that audible puff of air (Jagersma
  §3.2.2). Greek transcriptions from the Hellenistic period write
  them with φ θ χ — the Greek letters for aspirates.
- **`b d g`** were **plain voiceless** stops — [p t k] (no aspiration,
  no voicing) — like Spanish *pico, tinto, gato* or French *pas, ton,
  go* (Jagersma §3.2.3). Only around 2000 BCE did they shift to actual
  voiced [b d g] in most environments.

Practical consequence: when reading Old Sumerian aloud, `lugal` is
closer to [luˈkal] than to English *gal*; `gud` ('ox') is closer to
[kuθ] than to English *good*. By the **Ur III period** (~2100–2000
BCE) `b d g` had become voiced in most positions, so post-Ur-III
pronunciation matches the transliteration better.

### 4.3 Word-final stops

The aspirated stops `p t k` are **almost entirely lost** in
syllable-final position (Jagersma §3.2.2). Only a few morphemes
preserve a final `k`: `ak` 'make', `lu₅.k` 'live', `ka.k` 'mouth',
`ensi₂.k` 'ruler', and the genitive `=ak`. Even those are unstable
— don't pronounce the `k` of `lugal-ak` as audible; the surface
form is essentially `lugal=a` by Ur III.

The plain `b d g` retain their voiceless pronunciation word-finally
even after the rest of the system shifted to voiced (Jagersma §3.2.3).
The Sumerian word `pa₅ sig` 'small canal' is written with κ (Greek
plain-voiceless) in Hellenistic-period transcriptions of the final
`g`.

### 4.4 The glottal stop `/ʾ/` (Jagersma §3.2.4)

A real phoneme in Old Sumerian, written `÷` in Jagersma. **By the Ur
III period the glottal stop had lost phonemic status** — it lingers
as predictable phonological glue but is no longer contrastive. In
**Oracc spelling it's usually unwritten** (sometimes `ʾ`).

Words that originally had an initial glottal stop:
- `an` 'heaven' → /ʾan/
- `ab` 'window' → /ʾab/
- `a` 'water' → /ʾaj/ (also with a `j`, see §6)

Grammatical morphemes with a glottal stop in their lexical form:
- nominalizer `{÷a}` (= `=a` on the surface)
- enclitic copula `{÷am}` (= `-am₃` on the surface)
- locative case `{÷a}` (= `=a` on the surface)

For practical reading-aloud: pronounce as a tiny catch at the
beginning of a vowel-initial word, or omit altogether. Even in Old
Sumerian, intervocalic glottal stops often contracted with adjacent
vowels (Jagersma §3.2.4): `/a÷e/` → /ā/ or /ē/.

## 5. The affricates `z` and `ř` (Jagersma §3.3)

### 5.1 `z` (Jagersma §3.3.1)

**Pronounced [ts]** — like the Italian *z* in *pizza*, or the German
*z* in *zwei*. NOT like English *z*.

The transliteration `z` is misleading. In the third millennium,
Sumerian `z` was a voiceless **dental affricate** [ts], the same
sound as Akkadian /s/ (which was an affricate too, before
de-affricating around 1500 BCE). Around 2000 BCE Sumerian `z` became
voiced [dz] in many environments, but stayed [ts] word-finally.

Examples:
- `zà-mí` 'praise / lyre' → [tsa miː]
- `zú-lum` 'date' → [tsu lum]
- `Ξισουθρος` (Berossos's Greek spelling of Sumerian `zi-u₄-sù-řá`)
  uses Greek `Ξ` /ks/ for `z`, retaining the affricate reading
  (Jagersma §3.3.1)

### 5.2 `ř` — the "Sumerian r" (Jagersma §3.3.2)

A separate phoneme from plain `r`. Jagersma writes it `ř`; **Oracc
does NOT distinguish — both `r` and `ř` are written `r` in our
transliteration**.

**Pronounced [tsʰ]** — the aspirated counterpart of `z`. (Same place
of articulation, same affricate manner, but with aspiration after the
release.)

The `ř` phoneme **disappeared as a distinct sound during the third
millennium** (Jagersma §3.3.2):

- **Syllable-finally**: lost completely by mid-third-millennium.
- **Before a vowel**: became `/r/` in Southern dialect (Lagash,
  Umma), became `/d/` in Northern dialect (Nippur, Isin, Ur,
  Garshana). The Southern reflex /r/ is what won out in "standard
  Neo-Sumerian" (Ur III).

Old Babylonian copies of literary texts often reflect the Northern
`/d/` reflex, while Ur III administrative texts reflect the Southern
`/r/` reflex. For Oracc transliterations: both are spelled `r`.

Words known to have had `ř` (Jagersma §3.3.2):
`bar` 'open' (= /baʰ/), `bu.r` 'tear out', `du₆.r` 'hill', `du₇.r`
'be perfect', `gu₄.r` 'bull', `keše₂.r` 'bind', `ki-en-ge.r` 'Sumer',
`ku₅.r` 'cut', `ře₆` 'bring', `sù.r` 'be far', `sukud` (= /sukuʰ/)
'high', `udu` (= /uʰa/) 'sheep', and others.

For reading aloud: pronounce all `r` as [ɾ] (a single tap, see §8).
This loses the `r/ř` distinction but the distinction is itself lost
in the historical period most readers care about.

## 6. The fricatives (Jagersma §3.4)

| Phoneme | Sound | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| /s/ | [s] | Plain dental sibilant. Like English *s* in *sit*. (Jagersma §3.4.1) |
| /š/ | [ʃ] | Like English *sh*. Written `š`; ASCII input `sh`. (Jagersma §3.4.2) |
| /ḫ/ | [x] | Voiceless velar fricative — like German *Bach*, Scottish *loch*, or the Russian х. Jagersma writes `ḫ` (sometimes typeset `h~`); Oracc writes `ḫ`. (Jagersma §3.4.3) |
| /h/ | [h] | Glottal fricative, like English *h* in *hot*. **Mostly lost as a phoneme by Ur III**; never written in standard Oracc transliteration but reconstructable for a few words: `á` 'arm' (/ha/), `é` 'house' (older /haj/), `íd` 'river', `áb` 'cow`, `lá` 'hang' (/lah/), `gada` 'linen' (/gadah/). (Jagersma §3.4.4) |

**Critical for Oracc readers**: `š` is consistently rendered as `š`
in Oracc (sometimes `sh` in ASCII input contexts). `ḫ` is
consistently `ḫ` (sometimes `h` in ASCII contexts where the
underdot/tilde isn't available). Plain `s` and `š` are kept apart by
the writing system; one of the worst bugs is reading `š` as plain
*s*.

Note that Old Akkadian had a `/ś/` phoneme that **merged with /š/**
during the Old Akkadian period (Jagersma §3.4.1), which is why many
Sumerian-into-Akkadian loanwords have `š` in their Akkadian form
even though the source Sumerian word had `/s/` (e.g. Akkadian *šangû*
'temple administrator' from Sumerian *saĝĝa*).

## 7. The nasals (Jagersma §3.5)

| Phoneme | Sound | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| /m/ | [m] | Like English. (Jagersma §3.5.2) |
| /n/ | [n] | Like English / Spanish dental [n̪]. Sometimes palatalized before front vowels in spellings like `(C)a-e` for `=ane`. (Jagersma §3.5.3) |
| /ŋ/ | [ŋ] | Like English *sing*, **but can appear word-initially**. Written `ŋ` in Oracc (Jagersma writes `ĝ`; ETCSL/older writes `j`). (Jagersma §3.5.4) |

The velar nasal `ŋ` is the most distinctive Sumerian sound for
English speakers — English allows [ŋ] only in word-final and medial
position. In Sumerian, `ŋ` can begin a word: `ŋar` 'place' starts
with the [ŋ] sound.

Words with initial `ŋ`:
- `ŋar` 'place' → [ŋaɾ]
- `ŋen` 'go' → [ŋen]
- `ŋen-na` 'gone' → [ŋenːa]
- `ŋeš` 'wood, sixty' → [ŋeʃ]
- `ŋir` (place name component, "foot") → [ŋiɾ]
- `ŋir₃-su` 'Girsu' (city name) → [ŋiɾsu]

To produce initial [ŋ]: practice ending an English word in *-ing*
(*sing*, *king*, *ring*) and then start a vowel from the [ŋ]
position without releasing. Or: think of "singing" but truncate the
first *si-*.

In **Akkadian loanwords from Sumerian**, `ŋ` is rendered with `g` or
`k` word-initially, `n` word-finally, `ng` intervocalically. Examples
(Jagersma §3.5.4):
- `ŋiš-ùr` 'beam' → Akk. *gušūru*
- `pisaŋ` 'basket' → Akk. *pišannu*
- `ur-saŋ` 'hero' → Akk. *uršānu*
- `saŋŋa` 'temple administrator' → Akk. *šangû*

So when Akkadian speakers heard Sumerian `ŋ`, they reached for the
closest Akkadian sounds — confirming that `ŋ` was indeed a velar
nasal.

`ŋ` was lost as a distinct phoneme during or after the Old
Babylonian period (Jagersma §3.5.4) — i.e. after Sumerian was no
longer spoken. In our period (Old Sumerian / Ur III) it's still a
real, audible nasal.

## 8. The liquids and glide (Jagersma §3.6–§3.8)

### 8.1 /l/ (Jagersma §3.6)

[l] — a voiced dental or alveolar lateral. Like Italian *l*, German
*l*, Spanish *l*, French *l*. **NOT the dark/velarized English /l/**
(as in *milk*). Use a "clear" or "light" /l/ throughout.

There is **only one /l/ phoneme** — Jagersma rules out the older
suggestion of two distinct laterals (§3.6).

### 8.2 /r/ (Jagersma §3.7)

[ɾ] — a **single-tap** (tap, NOT trill), like the *r* in Spanish
*pero* 'but' or the medial *t/d* in American English *butter, ladder*.
Distinct from the trilled multi-tap [r] of Italian or Spanish *rr*.

This explains why Sumerian /r/ and /d/ were sometimes confused
(Jagersma §3.7, §7.11.1): a single-tap [ɾ] is acoustically very close
to a [d], and the scribes occasionally substituted one for the other
in word-final position.

### 8.3 /j/ (Jagersma §3.8)

[j] — like English *y* in *yes*, Spanish *y* in *yo*.

This phoneme was **progressively lost** during the third millennium
and was **gone by the Ur III period**. Original /j/-initial words
are now rendered with an initial vowel:

- `a` 'water' = older /ʾaj/
- `é` 'house' = older /haj/ → /ʾē/
- `i` 'five' = older /ji/
- `u` 'ten' = older /ju/
- `è` 'go out' = older /je/
- `íl` 'lift' = older /jil/
- `en` 'lord' = older /jen/

For reading aloud: use the surface vowel-initial form
(`a`, `é`, etc.). The /j/ is preserved in our notes only for
historical purposes.

## 9. Vowels (Jagersma §3.9)

### 9.1 Short vowels (Jagersma §3.9.1)

Four short phonemes: **/a/, /e/, /i/, /u/**.

Approximate values:
- /a/ ≈ [a] — low unrounded back, Spanish *padre*
- /e/ ≈ [e] — low front, Italian *bene*
- /i/ ≈ [i] — high front, Spanish *si*
- /u/ ≈ [u] — high rounded back, Spanish *luna*

For Old Sumerian vowel-harmony purposes (§3.9.3), the vowels split
into **two height classes**:
- **Low**: /a/, /e/
- **High**: /i/, /u/

Akkadian-speaking scribes had trouble keeping /e/ and /i/ apart
because Akkadian's /e/ was marginal (Jagersma §3.9.1). This means
**our transliterations may show /i/ where Sumerian actually had /e/**,
particularly for word signs read from late lexical lists.

### 9.2 Long vowels (Jagersma §3.9.2)

**Vowel length is phonemic** — i.e. Sumerian had both /a, e, i, u/
short and /ā, ē, ī, ū/ long, and the difference could distinguish
words. The writing system did sometimes mark length (e.g. signs like
`ne` for /nē/ vs `né` for /nĕ/, or by plene spelling `ge-e-en₆`), but
inconsistently.

**Oracc spelling does NOT systematically mark length.** When in
doubt, treat written vowels as short. The few cases where length is
marked:
- Plene spellings: `e-e-` for /ē/
- Specialized "long" signs: rarer in Old Sumerian

Vowel length arose from:
- Contractions of two short vowels (e.g. /a÷e/ → /ē/, /a÷a/ → /ā/)
- Compensatory lengthening before reduced ventive /m-/ etc.
- Inherited length in some stems

### 9.3 Old Sumerian vowel harmony (Jagersma §3.9.3)

In Old Sumerian only (~24–25th c. BCE in Lagash), the vocalic prefix
`{÷i}` and several other prefixes surface as /e/ before a syllable
with /e/ or /a/, and as /i/ before a syllable with /i/ or /u/.

```
e-né-ŋar    /÷i-nni-n-ŋar/   'he put it on him' — /÷i/ → [e] before /e/
ì-dú-da-a   /÷i-n-du-÷a=÷a/  'when she had given birth'  — /÷i/ → [i] before /u/
```

This rule **disappeared by Ur III**. It's a useful **dating
diagnostic**: Old Sumerian texts from Lagash spell the prefix `e-`
before low-vowel syllables; Ur III texts always spell it `i₃-`.

### 9.4 Vowel changes (Jagersma §3.9.4)

- **Assimilation**: in polysyllabic stems, vowels often assimilate
  to the vowel in an adjacent syllable. Examples:
  - `ḫu-luḫ` ← *ḫa-luḫ* 'be afraid'
  - `silim` ← *salim* 'be intact'
  - `libir` ← *labir* 'of old'
  - `gada` ← *gida* 'linen'
- **Initial-vowel loss** in words of 3+ syllables under specific
  stress conditions (see §10):
  - `dè-na` ← `eden=ak` 'of the plain'
  - `ti-da` ← `iti.d-a` 'of the month'
- **/u/ → /i/ after /r/**: `bara₂-ri` ← earlier `bara₂-ru` 'set up a
  dais'.
- **/aj/ → /ē/**: documented for `é` 'house' (older /haj/) and `a`
  'water' (older /ʾaj/, sometimes transliterated `e₄`).

## 10. Syllable structure and stress (Jagersma §3.10, §3.11)

### 10.1 Syllable structure (Jagersma §3.10)

**Strictly CV or CVC.** Every syllable starts with a consonant; no
syllable-initial vowels (the apparent vowel-initial words begin with
/ʾ/, /h/, or /j/ historically). No consonant clusters at syllable
boundaries except a small number of medial geminations (§3.10).

Sequences that *look* like vowel hiatuses in Oracc spelling
(`na-e-a`, `e-a`) are typically defective spellings of forms with
underlying glottal stops or other consonants (`/nabbe÷a/`,
`/ʾe÷a/`).

### 10.2 Stress (Jagersma §3.11)

**Sumerian had a fixed final-syllable stress**: every phonological
word is stressed on its **last syllable**.

Evidence: **all Sumerian loanwords in Akkadian are stressed on the
last syllable** of the source Sumerian word (Jagersma §3.11, citing
Keetman 2005). This puts Sumerian in the company of ~10% of the
world's languages with fixed final stress.

Practical reading:
- `lugal` → [luˈgal] (with [g] = [k] in older Old Sumerian; see §4)
- `dingir` → [dinˈgiɾ] — note the [ɾ], not English /r/
- `é-gal` → [ʔeːˈgal] 'palace' (from earlier /haj.kal/)
- `gu-de₂-a` → [gu.deˈʔa] 'Gudea'

Long, polysyllabic compound words get stress on the very last syllable
of the phonological word — so `e-ne-éŋ` 'word' is [eneˈeŋ].

### 10.3 Stress and vowel loss

Word-initial vowels in **long polysyllabic words** (3+ syllables) are
often deleted because they're unstressed pretonic — this is how
forms like `dè-na` from `eden=ak` arise (Jagersma §3.11). This is
**not** the strong reduction of German or Russian; Sumerian doesn't
reduce unstressed vowels to schwa, it just occasionally loses an
initial vowel in a long word.

## 11. Period-specific drift

Pronunciation was not stable across the 500 years Jagersma describes
(2500–2000 BCE). Major shifts:

### 11.1 By the late 25th century (mid-Old Sumerian)

- /ř/ already lost word-finally (Jagersma §3.3.2): `bař` is being
  written `ba` already
- Glottal stop already assimilating to preceding consonants
  (Jagersma §3.2.4)
- Vowel harmony rule active in Lagash texts (Jagersma §3.9.3)

### 11.2 In the Old Akkadian period (~23rd c.)

- /ś/–/š/ merger in Akkadian (affecting loanwords in/out)
  (Jagersma §3.4.1)
- /ř/ becoming /r/ (South) or /d/ (North) before a vowel
  (Jagersma §3.3.2)
- Vowel-harmony rule beginning to dissolve in some areas

### 11.3 By the time of Gudea (~22nd c., Lagash II)

- /t/ → /r/ between vowels in the ablative prefix `{ta}`
  (Jagersma §3.2.2, §19.3.1)
- /h/ already nearly gone as an independent phoneme
  (Jagersma §3.4.4)

### 11.4 By the Ur III period (~21st c.)

- /b d g/ have shifted from plain voiceless [p t k] to voiced [b d g]
  in most environments (Jagersma §3.2.3)
- Glottal stop is no longer phonemic
- /j/ has been lost as an independent phoneme
- /h/ gone
- Vowel harmony rule completely gone
- Genitive `=ak` has lost its final /k/ entirely before consonants

### 11.5 By the Old Babylonian period (~19–17th c.)

- /ŋ/ being lost (or at least conflated with /n/ or /g/ in spelling)
- Sumerian no longer a living vernacular
- Pronunciation increasingly Akkadianized

### 11.6 In the Hellenistic period (~3rd c. BCE)

Sumerian and Akkadian both still being read aloud in cultic and
scholarly contexts, but pronounced through a heavy Akkadian–Greek
filter. Greek transcriptions from this period are our best evidence
for the Hellenistic-era pronunciation of stops and affricates.

For agent-translation purposes: **pronounce ED-period Sumerian
following the third-millennium reconstructions** above (e.g. plain
voiceless /b d g/ as [p t k], aspirated /p t k/ as [pʰ tʰ kʰ]). For
Ur III register, the modern romanization is closer to surface
phonetics.

## 12. Reading a Sumerian line aloud — worked examples

### 12.1 ED royal-inscription line

Original (Old Sumerian, Enmetena, ED IIIb, ~24th c. BCE):
```
kur-kur e-ma-ḫuŋ
'He hired the foreign lands for himself.'
```

Pronunciation (Old Sumerian, ~24th c.):
- `kur-kur` → [kʰuɾ kʰuɾ] — with aspirated initial /k/ and tap /ɾ/
- `e-ma-ḫuŋ` → [ema xuŋ] (or with VH: vowel-harmony rule applied to
  the prefix vowel) — note the velar fricative /x/ for ḫ, the velar
  nasal /ŋ/ at the end

Final stress on the last syllable of each phonological word: [kʰuɾ
kʰuˈɾ ema ˈxuŋ].

### 12.2 Ur III administrative line

```
gu₄ niga gu₃-de₂-a ensi₂ lagas{ki}-ka-ke₄ mu-na-šum₂
```

Pronunciation (Ur III, ~21st c.):
- `gu₄` → [gu] (now voiced /g/; the final /ř/ already lost)
- `niga` → [niga] (now voiced)
- `gu₃-de₂-a` → [gu de ʔa] — Gudea's name; voiced /g/ and /d/ by Ur III
- `ensi₂` → [ensi] (with final /k/ lost in surface form)
- `lagas{ki}` → [lagas] — the {ki} determinative is silent (Jagersma
  §2.2)
- `mu-na-šum₂` → [mu na ʃum] — voiced m/n; /š/ as [ʃ]

Final stress per phonological word.

### 12.3 Word `dingir` 'god'

Transliteration `dingir`. The signs are `DINGIR` (or `AN` read as
`dingir`). Pronunciation: `[dinˈgiɾ]`. The final `r` is a tap [ɾ],
not the English approximant.

### 12.4 Word `lugal` 'king'

Transliteration `lugal`. Old Sumerian pronunciation:
- ED IIIa (early 25th c.): [luˈkal] — with plain voiceless /g/ = [k]
- Ur III (~21st c.): [luˈgal] — with voiced /g/ = [g]

The cuneiform sign is 𒈗. Stress on the final syllable in both periods.

## 13. What we do NOT know

Jagersma is explicit about the limits of phonological reconstruction
(§3.1, §3.11). Things we have no reliable data on:

- **Most stem vowels written with word signs.** We "read" them
  through Akkadian lexical lists from the second millennium; the
  actual Sumerian vowel may have been different.
- **Most stem vowel lengths.** Word signs hide length distinctions.
- **Pitch / intonation.** Sumerian may have had pitch accent (§3.11
  speculates "perhaps a stress-accent rather than such a strong one
  as in German, but rather one as in Italian or Spanish"). Tone or
  pitch contrasts have been proposed by some scholars but Jagersma
  does not accept them (§3.1).
- **Exact phonetic value of /ḫ/.** Defined as a voiceless velar
  fricative (Jagersma §3.4.3), but the precise place may have shifted
  over time.
- **Whether /r/ and /ř/ ever co-occurred in living speech**, or
  whether /ř/ was already a relic by the documented period.
- **Phonemic status of /h/ after the Old Sumerian period** — by Ur
  III it had been lost (Jagersma §3.4.4).
